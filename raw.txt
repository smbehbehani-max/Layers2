2.1 1. Bahra 1
Arabic name: ١ ‫بحرة‬
Record ID: bahra-1
Category: archaeological
Subcategory: Ubaid Settlement
Latitude (N): 29.6363
Longitude (E): 47.9552
Period: Ubaid Period
Date range: 5500–4900 BC
Elevation: 12.30–14.50 m a.s.l.
Scientific description: The largest Ubaid-period settlement discovered outside Mesopotamia. Bahra 1
is a sprawling site in the Al-Subiyah desert that served as a major center for shell ornament production
and trade during the late 6th millennium BC. Rutkowski & Kiersnowski (2025) established that during the
Ubaid period, the coastline was 1.5 km south of Bahra 1, and the site lay further inland from the sea than
the fishing village of H3. The Bahra anticline — a domal structure related to Precambrian Hormuz salt
tectonics — has caused tectonic uplift of 1.72–2.64 mm/yr in this area.
Adult-content description: Bahra 1 is one of the most significant Ubaid-period settlements discovered
outside Mesopotamia. Located on the Al-Subiyah coast north of Kuwait Bay, discovered in 2009 by the
Kuwaiti-Polish Archaeological Mission. Rutkowski & Kiersnowski (2025) established that during the Ubaid
2/3 period, the site lay inland from the coast, while H3 occupied a narrow peninsula closer to the sea. The
settlement contains multi-room stone architecture, extensive Ubaid pottery assemblages, and evidence of
long-distance trade networks extending to Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley.

Significance: Largest Ubaid settlement outside Mesopotamia; evidence of the earliest cultural exchange
networks between Southern Mesopotamia and Eastern Arabia across the Arabian Gulf. Radiocarbon dating
of marine shells from the paleo-seabed confirms the mid-Holocene highstand reached this area.
Key discoveries
• Shell Crafting Workshop: A 7,700-year-old courtyard used for crafting shell ornaments, the oldest known
specialized workshop of its kind in the Gulf region.
• Ancient Kilns: More than 20 ancient kilns dating to approximately 7,700 years ago, providing evidence
of early pyrotechnology and pottery production.
• Owl Sculpture: A rare ancient owl sculpture, one of the earliest artistic representations found in the
Arabian Peninsula.
• Copper Pin Fragment: An extremely rare copper pin fragment, evidence of early metallurgy and longdistance trade with copper-producing regions.
• Ubaid Pottery: Distinctive painted pottery with geometric patterns characteristic of the Ubaid culture,
linking this site to Mesopotamian traditions.
• Paleo-Seabed Shells: Radiocarbon-dated marine mollusk shells from the paleo-seabed south of Bahra 1,
confirming the mid-Holocene highstand reached this area around 5700 BP (Rutkowski & Kiersnowski,
2025).
• One of the largest collections of Ubaid pottery outside Mesopotamia
• Multi-room structures with stone foundations — earliest permanent architecture in the Arabian Gulf
• Extensive lithic assemblage including blades, scrapers, and projectile points
• Imported obsidian and bitumen demonstrating connections to Mesopotamia
Excavation / field mission: Kuwaiti-Polish Archaeological Mission (KPAM), Polish Centre of

2.2 2. H3 (Jazirat Dubaji)
Arabic name: H3 ‫جزيرة دوباجي‬
Record ID: h3-site
Category: archaeological
Subcategory: Ubaid Maritime Site
Latitude (N): 29.6417
Longitude (E): 48.1506
Period: Ubaid Period
Date range: 5500–5000 BC
Elevation: 5.00–6.50 m a.s.l.
Scientific description: H3 is located on Jazirat Dubaji, a low peninsula on the north side of Kuwait Bay.
During the Ubaid 2/3 period (turn of the 6th and 5th millennia BC), the sea bordered this fishing village
on a narrow peninsula, while Bahra 1 lay further inland (Rutkowski & Kiersnowski, 2025). The site is a
cultural borderland between Mesopotamian and Arabian Neolithic traditions, yielding some of the most
significant maritime archaeological finds in human history.
Adult-content description: H3 is located on Jazirat Dubaji, a low peninsula on the north side of Kuwait
Bay. During the Ubaid 2/3 period, the sea bordered this fishing village on a narrow peninsula. The site is
a cultural borderland between Mesopotamian and Arabian Neolithic traditions, yielding some of the most
significant maritime archaeological finds in human history.
Significance: Contains the world’s earliest evidence for seagoing vessels and the oldest evidence for the
use of masts and sails, fundamentally changing our understanding of prehistoric maritime technology.
Key discoveries
• World’s Earliest Boat Evidence: Bitumen pieces with barnacles on one side and reed impressions on the
other — the earliest physical evidence for actual boats in Western Asia and seagoing vessels globally.
• Reed-Bundle Boat Model: A detailed ceramic model of a reed-bundle boat with incisions mimicking reed
bundles, more detailed than any similar Mesopotamian find.
• Oldest Sail Evidence: A ceramic disc depicting a reed-bundle boat with two masts — the oldest evidence
for the use of masts and sails in human history.
• Date Stones: Among the earliest evidence of date palm cultivation, connecting to similar finds on Dalma
Island in the UAE.

2.3 3. Al-Subiyah Tumuli Cemetery
Arabic name: ‫مقبرة تلال الصبية‬
Record ID: subiyah-tumuli
Category: archaeological

Subcategory: Bronze Age Cemetery
Latitude (N): 29.645
Longitude (E): 48.05
Period: Bronze Age
Date range: 2900–1150 BC
Scientific description: A vast tumulus burial field stretching across the northern coast of Kuwait Bay,
containing approximately 130 stone burial mounds and associated structures. Rutkowski & Kiersnowski
(2025) established that tumuli are distributed along terraces parallel to the former sea bay and the Jal azZor hills, with most positioned above 18 m a.s.l. Radiocarbon dates concentrate from the end of the 4th
millennium to the mid-2nd millennium BC, coinciding with the highest sea levels in Kuwait Bay. The
absence of known Bronze Age settlements suggests the burial mounds were constructed by nomadic or
semi-nomadic groups with close ties to the sea.
Adult-content description: A vast tumulus burial field with 130 stone burial mounds on the northern
coast of Kuwait Bay. Rutkowski & Kiersnowski (2025) established that tumuli are distributed along terraces
parallel to the former sea bay, most above 18 m a.s.l. The absence of known Bronze Age settlements
suggests construction by nomadic or semi-nomadic groups with close ties to the sea.
Significance: One of the largest Bronze Age burial complexes in the Arabian Gulf. Artefact-based dating
spans from Early Dynastic I-II ( 2900 BC) through the Kassite period ( 1150 BC), with possible earlier
Ubaid-period origins and later Iron Age reuse.
Key discoveries
• Tumuli with Outer Ring Walls: Impressive dry-stone funerary monuments with distinctive outer ring
walls, a construction technique unique to this region.
• Semi-Precious Stone Beads: Perforated pearls and beads of carnelian, agate, lapis lazuli, and chrysoprase
— evidence of long-distance trade reaching Afghanistan and the Indus Valley.
• Dilmun Shell Ornaments: Circular shell plaques with geometric decoration and ‘dot-in-circle’ motifs
characteristic of the Umm an-Nar and Dilmun cultures.
• Akkadian-Style Cylinder Seal: A cylinder seal dated 2300–2100 BC found between SBH 34 and Bahra 1,
connecting the cemetery to Mesopotamian administrative traditions.
• Kassite-Style Goblet: A goblet from SBH 34 dated 1600–1150 BC, evidence of continued use of the
cemetery into the Late Bronze Age.

• Circular shell plaques with “dot-in-circle” motifs of Umm an-Nar and Dilmun cultures

2.4 4. Enigmatic Stone Platforms
Arabic name: ‫المنصات الحجر ية الغامضة‬
Record ID: stone-platforms
Category: archaeological
Subcategory: Ritual / Ceremonial Structures
Latitude (N): 29.638
Longitude (E): 48.08
Period: Bronze Age
Date range: 3000–1500 BC (inferred from sea-level alignment)
Elevation: 11.30–19.11 m a.s.l.
Scientific description: A survey in Al-Subiyah identified 21 linear stone platforms along 16 km of
coastline, making them the second most common stone structures after tumuli. These platforms are
typically 6–21 m long and less than 2 m wide, with rounded ends, flat tops, and a curb of vertical stone slabs.
One end is often broader and rounded, while the other resembles a boat’s bow or parabolic tip, giving them
a silhouette akin to a longboat or spearhead when viewed from above (Rutkowski & Kiersnowski, 2025).
Adult-content description: Boat-shaped stone platforms found along the Al-Subiyah coast, oriented
toward the ancient shoreline. These enigmatic structures are unique to the Kuwait Bay region with no
direct parallels elsewhere. Their boat-like shape and coastal orientation suggest a ritual or navigational
function related to maritime activities or funerary practices.
Significance: Their deliberate seaward orientation — perpendicular to the ancient coastline — suggests
they were built during the Bronze Age highstand when the sea was visible from their location. They may

represent funerary biers, ceremonial podia, or eschatological symbols of passage to the afterlife, evoking
Dilmun’s association with paradise in Sumerian mythology.
Key discoveries
• Boat-Shaped Silhouette: Elongated platforms with a distinct ‘bow end’ and broader ‘stern end’,
resembling longboats when viewed from above — possibly symbolizing the passage to the afterlife.
• Sunrise-Sunset Orientation: In the Bahra micro-region, five platforms are aligned east-west with the
narrow end pointing westward, possibly reflecting afterlife beliefs.
• Fan-Like Coastal Arrangement: Elsewhere, platforms follow a fan-like arrangement perpendicular to
the former shoreline, with narrow ends facing the sea.
• Association with Tumuli: Often found near tumuli, suggesting functional, ritual, or symbolic associations

2.5 5. Shell Midden SMQ 24 (Mughaira)
Arabic name: ‫ كومة الأصداف‬SMQ 24
Record ID: shell-midden-smq24
Category: archaeological
Subcategory: Shell Processing Site
Latitude (N): 29.643
Longitude (E): 48.02
Period: Ubaid to Late Bronze Age
Date range: 5000–1500 BC
Elevation: 11.00–11.50 m a.s.l.
Scientific description: An isolated shell midden approximately 20×20 m, composed almost exclusively
of Conus persicus shells, discovered at the base of Terrace A in the Mughaira area. The site served as
a shell processing location for bead production. Nearby finds include Ubaid pottery fragments and flint

pieces. KPAM discovered oval quartz pebbles among the shells, resembling the ‘chipped quartz industry’
identified at Bahra 1 and H3 (Rutkowski & Kiersnowski, 2025).
Adult-content description: SMQ 24 is a significant shell midden site near Mughaira on the Al-Subiyah
coast. Shell middens are ancient refuse heaps primarily composed of discarded mollusk shells, providing
invaluable evidence of prehistoric diet, seasonality, and coastal resource exploitation.
Significance: Radiocarbon dating yielded a surprisingly late date (Late Bronze Age), suggesting prolonged
or intermittent use across different periods. Located 3 km NW of H3 and 5.7 km east of Bahra 1, at
a comparable elevation, suggesting potential existence of other Ubaid-period sites along the prehistoric
coastline.
Key discoveries
• Conus persicus Shell Concentration: Thousands of C. persicus shells — the raw material for ancient bead
production — concentrated in a 20×20 m area.
• Oval Quartz Pebbles: Whole, halved, or fragmented quartz pebbles resembling the ‘chipped quartz
industry’ from Bahra 1 and H3, supporting the bead production hypothesis.
• Ubaid Pottery Fragment: A fragment of Ubaid pottery found nearby, potentially linking the midden to
the Ubaid-period settlements.
• Stratified shell deposits indicating multiple occupation phases
• Evidence of systematic shellfish harvesting
• Associated stone tools and pottery fragments
• Shell bead production workshop evidence
